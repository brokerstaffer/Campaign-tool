Part 01

Orientation

What this dashboard is, how it is laid out, and the handful of ideas that make every other screen easier to read.

What it is for

Every campaign, every email, every reply and every result lives inside EmailBison. That is where the sending happens, and it stays the single source of truth. What it does not give you is a way to ask questions — to slice a quarter by client, put two subject lines side by side, or see how much sending it takes to earn one interview.

This dashboard is that layer. It reads everything out of EmailBison on a schedule, arranges it by client, campaign, sequence step and individual email, and lets you filter all of it by date and by whoever you are reporting on. It also writes back: you can pause and resume campaigns, edit a sequence, and roll a winning sequence out to other campaigns without leaving the page.

The one rule that explains the rest: EmailBison is always the authority on what happened. This dashboard never invents a number. When something here looks different from EmailBison's own screen, the reason is always a difference in question — a date range, or a filter, or counting people rather than emails — never a difference in truth.

Signing in

The dashboard is private. You sign in once with your email address and password, and stay signed in for several days on that browser. Everyone on the team has their own login rather than sharing one. Nothing is public, and there is no way for anyone outside the team to reach any screen.

The four places you can go

The narrow strip down the left is the whole navigation. Four destinations, all of them live:

Analytics has four tabs across the top, and underneath them a filter bar that applies to whichever tab you are on. The first tab, Campaign, is itself split into four sub-views, chosen with the segmented control just below the headline numbers.

Sharing what you are looking at

Everything you set — the date range, the campaigns, the clients, the chosen sub-view, even which lines are on the chart — is written into the address bar. Three useful consequences:

  • Any view is a link. Copy the address, paste it into a message, and the person opening it sees exactly what you saw.
  • The back button works properly. It steps back through your filter changes rather than jumping out of the dashboard.
  • Reloading keeps your place. Refreshing never resets you to a default month.

Ticking this box does two things at once. Every headline tile grows a small change figure underneath it, and the chart gains a faded second line showing the earlier period drawn over the current one.

Part 03

The headline numbers

Twelve figures in two rows of six, sitting directly under the filter bar on the Campaign tab. They are the summary of everything below them.

The first row — volume

Sent

Emails delivered out of the door in the selected range.

Prospects

Distinct people contacted in the range. One person contacted three times counts once.

Replies

Every reply received, including automatic ones. Bounce notifications are excluded — they are not replies.

Human Replies

Replies with a person behind them. Out-of-office notes and auto-responders are taken out.

Positive

Replies marked as interested. This is the figure the whole operation is trying to grow.

Bounces

Emails that failed to land. Underlined when it is not zero, so it is never something you scroll past.

The second row — rates and timing

Reply Rate

How often an email earns any reply at all.Replies ÷ Sent

Human Rate

The same, counting only real people. Always lower than Reply Rate.Human Replies ÷ Sent

Positive Rate

Of the people who replied, how many were interested. Measured against replies, not sends.Positive ÷ Replies

Lead to Email

How many emails it takes to earn one positive reply, shown as a ratio such as 1 : 700.Sent ÷ Positive

Median Reply Time

How long a typical prospect takes to reply after the first email reaches them.

Median Follow-up Time

How long we take to answer once someone replies. A measure of the team, not the copy.

Why median and not average

A median is the typical case: half were faster, half slower. An average is dragged upward by the one person who replied three weeks later, which makes it a worse description of a normal day. On the Campaigns table you can add the average alongside the median — a wide gap between the two is itself a finding.

Reading a tile

  • Numbers are rounded here on purpose. The band shows 272.4K where the tables show 272,389. The band is for scanning; the tables are for comparing.
  • A dash means "no data", not zero. "No replies yet" and "replies answered instantly" must not look alike, so anything that cannot be worked out shows – rather than a confident 0.
  • Change your filters and the tiles go quiet, not stale. Each tile shows a placeholder while the new figure loads, so you are never reading last month's number under this month's filter.
  • With Compare on, each tile gains a small change figure. Green is an improvement, red is a decline — and for bounces and reply times, an improvement means going down.
Part 04

Charts

The day-by-day shape of the operation. Six things can be drawn, in any combination, over any range.

The six lines

Above the chart sits a row of chips, one per line. Click to add or remove. Each carries its own colour dot, and that colour belongs to the line permanently:

Sent Prospects Replies Human Positive Bounces

Turning Replies off does not repaint Human. Colour follows the thing being measured, never its position in the list, so the chart you are looking at now and the one in last week's screenshot use the same colours for the same lines.

The controls above the chart

Chart controls
ControlWhat it does
Volume / Rates Volume plots counts — emails, replies, people. Rates plots percentages, which lets a small campaign and a large one be compared on the same axis.
Normalize Rescales every line so its own peak sits at the top. Use it when one line is so much larger that the others are flat against the bottom — it compares shape rather than size. The tooltip keeps showing the real numbers.
Exclude weekends Removes Saturdays and Sundays from the horizontal axis. Almost nothing sends at the weekend, so leaving them in draws a saw-tooth that hides the trend. It changes the chart only — the headline tiles above are untouched.

Reading the chart

  • Hover anywhere and a vertical crosshair follows the pointer. The tooltip lists every active line for that day, not just the one nearest the cursor, so you never have to hunt for the second number.
  • With Compare on, the earlier period is drawn faded and dashed underneath. The tooltip names the actual date it is comparing against, so you are never guessing which day is which.
  • If a refresh fails, the last good chart stays on screen and a short message appears. A blank chart and a broken chart look identical, and they mean opposite things.
One deliberate quirk

Adding up the daily Prospects line gives a larger number than the Prospects tile. That is correct: someone contacted on Monday and again on Friday appears on both days in the chart, but is one person over the range. The chart counts contacts per day; the tile counts people. Every other line adds up exactly.

Part 05

Clients

The same metrics as the headline band, one row per client. This is the view you open when someone asks how a particular account is doing.

The table

Each row carries the client's name, how many campaigns they run, and then the familiar figures: Sent, Prospects, Replies, Positive, Reply %, Positive %, Lead:Email, Bounces and Median Reply. Sorted by volume, and every column can be sorted. A totals band sits at the foot.

Client is only a grouping. Every metric is worked out exactly the same way it is in the headline band — nothing is redefined for this view, so a client's Reply Rate here and the Reply Rate you get by filtering to that client are the same number.

The Unassigned row

Clients do not exist inside EmailBison. A campaign belongs to a client because the client's name appears in the campaign's name, and that match is made on the Clients page. When a campaign matches nobody, it lands in an Unassigned row rather than being quietly dropped.

That row is deliberate and it matters. Without it, the client rows would add up to less than the headline total by however much volume failed to match, and the table would be quietly wrong with nothing on screen to say so. With it, the totals always tie out — and Unassigned becomes a visible to-do list rather than an invisible error.

If you see a large Unassigned row: open the Clients page. It lists exactly which campaigns matched nothing, and assigning one takes two clicks.
Part 06

Campaigns

The deepest view in the dashboard. It starts as one row per campaign and opens all the way down to the text of an individual email.

Choosing what to see

The header reads Campaign 183 and 20 columns ▾. The column button opens a searchable list with Show all and Hide all, grouped so you can turn a whole family on at once. Your choice is remembered for next time.

The column groups
GroupColumns
VolumeSent, Prospects, Replies, Bounces
RatesReply %, Human %, Positive %, Bounce %, Hard bounces, Soft (delay) bounces, Hard %, Lead:Email
Reply SentimentPositive and neutral counts
Reply SourceHuman replies and bot replies, side by side
TimingMedian Reply, Average Reply, Median Follow-up
EventsIntros, Phone Screens, Interviews, Hires, total Outcomes, Emails per Intro, Emails per Hire

The campaign name stays pinned to the left as you scroll sideways, so you never lose track of which row you are reading.

Hard and soft bounces

EmailBison reports one bounce figure. This dashboard splits it by reading the bounce notice itself:

  • Hard — the address genuinely failed. Suppress it.
  • Soft (delay) — the receiving server was temporarily busy and will retry. Not a failure.

This is worth knowing because roughly one bounce in seven is a delay, which means the headline bounce rate is meaningfully higher than the rate of mail that actually failed. That is the difference between a domain in trouble and a receiving server having a slow morning. Hard and soft will not add up exactly to the Bounces column — a small number of bounces arrive with no notice attached — and the columns are named after what they are rather than pretending otherwise.

Opening a campaign

Rows open in three levels:

  1. The campaign. Its totals for the selected range.
  2. Its steps. Each email in the sequence, in order, with the wait before it and its own numbers. Variants sit nested underneath the step they replace.
  3. The email itself. Opening a step shows what actually gets sent.

The email panel has three views. Preview shows the message as a recipient receives it, with one version of any spun text chosen — press Shuffle to roll another. Spintax shows the raw text with every alternative highlighted, so you can see all the variations at once. Blueprint breaks the message into its working parts — greeting, opening, proof, call to action, sign-off — and shows the actual line it picked for each, so you can check the split rather than trust it.

Steps and variants

A step is a position in the sequence. A variant is an alternative wording for a position that already exists. Three steps with one variant on the third is a three-step sequence, and it is counted that way everywhere in the dashboard — in this table, on the campaign page, on the offer cards, and in the dialog that copies a sequence elsewhere.

A row therefore reads 3 steps · 1 variant, never "4 steps". Waits are shown as the gap before a step — sends immediately for the first, then 1d after step 1 — because the first email goes out the moment a lead enters the sequence, and printing a delay against it would claim a wait that never happens. A variant never shows a wait of its own: it inherits its parent's position.

Medals

Where a step has more than one variant, the best performer earns 🥇, then 🥈 and 🥉. Ranking is by positive rate, with reply rate breaking a tie.

A medal is only awarded once a variant has sent at least 200 emails. Without a floor, a variant with three sends and one positive reply wins every time, and a gold medal on a sample of three is worse than no medal at all.

Logging an outcome

A reply is not the end of the story. From any reply you can record what happened next — an introduction, a phone screen, an interview, a hire — and it is credited to that campaign from then on, appearing in the Events columns and on the Attribution tab. Logged outcomes can be removed again if one is recorded by mistake.

Part 08

Infrastructure

The health of the sending estate — around 1,470 inboxes across 150 sending domains. This tab answers one question: is anything drifting toward a reputation problem?

The headline, and why it is not enough

The large figure is the bounce rate across everything. It is a useful number and a misleading one on its own: an average over 150 domains cannot tell you whether you have a broad problem or two bad apples.

So directly beside it, the estate is split into three bands. These are cold-email industry thresholds, not house rules — sustained bounce above 3% is what gets a sending domain flagged.

The three bands
BandBounce rateWhat it means
HealthyUnder 2%Nothing to do.
Watch2% – 3%Not yet a problem, but heading for one. Worth knowing how much volume sits here.
CriticalOver 3%Act now. Reputation damage compounds.

Each band reports both how many domains are in it and how much sending they carry. Those are different facts and the second is usually the important one: "two domains are bad" and "a third of your volume is drifting toward the line" call for very different responses.

Three ways to look at it

Views
ViewUse it when
By domainThe default. Reputation is managed at the domain, so this is where most decisions get made.
By providerComparing across mailbox providers, to see whether one is behaving worse than the rest.
By inboxThe individual mailbox — its address, provider, daily limit and status. Not filtered by volume, so you can find an inbox that has never sent, which is itself a finding.

Each view has a search box and can be sorted by volume, bounce rate or reply rate.

Needs attention

A separate list, sorted so the worst bounce rate is at the top rather than the biggest sender. One bad inbox can be found without scrolling past twenty healthy ones.

It ignores anything that has sent fewer than fifty emails. A bounce rate over three sends is noise — a single bounce is 33% and means nothing. Fifty is low enough to catch a newly warming inbox going bad and high enough that the list is worth reading.

Part 09

Attribution

What happened after the reply. Introductions, phone screens, interviews and hires — and which campaigns earned them.

Where the outcomes come from

Outcomes arrive from a separate results feed, and that feed covers more than EmailBison. Each outcome is one of three things, and the top of the tab reports the split honestly:

How each outcome is classified
ClassificationWhat it means
Credited to a campaignThe feed named an EmailBison campaign. This is the only kind that appears in any campaign's numbers.
Another platformThe feed named an Instantly campaign. Real result, not ours to claim.
No campaign foundAdded directly by the client with no campaign attached.
Not yet resolvedStill being matched up. Will move into one of the categories above.
Why this care is taken

The same people are in both systems. Matching an Instantly result to an EmailBison campaign by email address would succeed — and would credit our campaign with another platform's work. It is also the kind of error that never gets caught, because it makes the numbers look better. So only an outcome the feed can prove belongs to an EmailBison campaign is ever credited to one.

What it costs to earn one

Four measures, each showing the count and how many emails it took:

Email to Introduction

Sending required per introduction.

Email to Phone Call

Counts both scheduled and held calls — the question is how much sending buys a conversation.

Email to Interview

Scheduled and held, on the same principle.

Email to Hire

The one that pays for everything else.

The funnel

Six stages in fixed progression: Introduction → Phone screen scheduled → Phone screen → Interview scheduled → Interview → Hired. The order is the real-world order and never re-sorts by size — a funnel sorted by size is a bar chart with extra steps.

Where people stopped

Three outcomes are deliberately kept out of the funnel: no show, rejected (either direction) and keep warm. These are not stages anyone passes through — they are where someone stopped. Putting them in the funnel would read as though hundreds of people advanced to "no show". They get their own section beneath it.

If a new kind of outcome starts arriving tomorrow that fits neither list, it still appears, under its own name. A silently dropped outcome is indistinguishable from an outcome that never happened.

Outcomes over time, and by campaign

  • Over time — outcomes by week, stacked by stage, so you can see results building rather than a single total. The most recent week is drawn faded and labelled as incomplete rather than dropped, because a part-finished week plotted as though it were finished looks like a collapse.
  • Which campaigns produce results — a table ranking campaigns by what they actually produced rather than by how much they sent. This is where a campaign with modest volume and excellent outcomes becomes visible.

Every outcome

The full list, filterable by outcome type and by source, showing the person, the campaign it was credited to, what happened and when. This is the record to open when a number needs explaining to someone.

Part 10

Copy & Offer

Which words work, and which offers work. Two related questions on one tab.

The seven ways an email is described

Comparing copy needs a vocabulary. Every opening email can be tagged along seven dimensions, each with suggested values you can extend:

Copy dimensions
DimensionDescribesTypical values
Subject Line TypeHow the subject earns the openVariable, Direct, Personalized, Question, Curiosity Gap
OpeningHow the first line approaches the readerCompliment, Observation, Mutual Connection, Problem, Direct Ask
PrepositionHow the offer is framedOpportunity, Comparison, Scarcity, Status, Savings
Social ProofWhat credibility is used, if anyNone, Named Client, Volume, Peer Group, Result
Call To ActionWhat the email asks forInterest Check, Book a Call, Reply Yes, Question, Soft Ask
Tone / StyleHow it soundsFormal, Casual, Direct, Warm, Blunt
StructureThe shape of the messageShort, Long, Bulleted, Narrative, Two-liner

Tagging is done on a campaign's own page, on its Copy & Offer tab. Untagged copy is never hidden — it appears as an Untagged row so you can see how much of the picture is still missing.

How the copy performs

The main table groups every campaign's opening email by the dimensions you have selected, and reports sends, replies, positives and bounces for each group. Start with one dimension; add more with + Add Dimension to cross them — "Question subject lines with a named client as proof" is a different and often better question than either on its own. Chips show what is active; remove one by clicking its ✕. The last one cannot be removed, because an empty selection has no table to show.

Rows are sorted by positive rate rather than volume, so the winner is at the top where you expect it. Coverage is stated above the table — how much of your sending is actually tagged — so a confident-looking ranking built on a fraction of the data cannot be mistaken for the whole picture.

The 500-send floor

Medals here need at least 500 sends behind a value before it can be ranked. Copy comparisons are the easiest place in the product to fool yourself, and without a floor the smallest sample wins every time.

Offers

An offer is what you are actually selling — the proposition, not the wording. Several campaigns usually run the same one. Each offer is a card showing:

  • Its name and niche, and how many campaigns run it.
  • A line naming the sequence behind it — 3 steps from Jeff Cook Real Estate… — which is a button. Clicking it opens the full sequence: every step in order, the waits between them, variants nested under the step they replace, and each one openable to its Preview, Spintax and Blueprint. You can read exactly what you are about to roll out without leaving the page.
  • Sent, Reply %, Positive % and Bounce % for the selected range.
  • A per-client split, shown only when the offer runs for more than one client — for a single-client offer it would just restate the line above.
  • Copy sequence to a campaign.

Suggested groups

Campaigns that open with the same email are the same offer, and the dashboard can see that without being told. Suggested groups are proposals built from the data — one group currently covers 36 campaigns on a single subject line. Naming one turns it into a real offer; that is the only part a person is genuinely needed for. These cards carry the same read-the-sequence button.

Rolling a sequence out

Once a sequence is proven, it can be copied into other campaigns in one pass. Pick the targets, then choose how:

Copy modes
ModeWhat happens
AppendThe copied steps are added after whatever is already there.
ReplaceThe target's existing sequence is swapped for this one. The new steps are created first and the old ones removed only once that has succeeded, so a failure halfway through never leaves a campaign with no sequence at all.

Before anything is written, a plan is shown for each target: exactly which steps will be created, with variants marked as variants and given no wait of their own. Anything that would be blocked is named and explained rather than silently skipped. As it runs, each campaign reports its own result, and any that failed can be retried on their own without redoing the ones that worked.

Part 11

Campaigns

The operational side. Everything so far has been reporting; this is where you change things.

The list

Finding a campaign
ControlWhat it does
SearchMatches on campaign name.
StatusDraft, active, paused, completed, archived — with a count beside each so you can see the shape of the workspace at a glance.
TagNarrows to campaigns carrying a particular tag.

Each row shows the campaign, its client, its status and its lifetime figures. A status the dashboard does not recognise is shown in red rather than quietly displayed as a draft — an unrecognised status means something changed upstream and it should be visible, not hidden.

Bulk actions

Tick several campaigns and pause, resume or archive them together. Three safeguards, all deliberate:

  1. It names its targets before it runs. The confirmation lists exactly which campaigns will be affected — not "12 campaigns".
  2. Each campaign reports its own result. If nine succeed and three fail, you are told which three and why, rather than getting one summary that hides it.
  3. A failure is a real failure. If EmailBison rejects a change, its own reason is shown. Nothing is reported as done unless it was done.

Actions that make no sense for a campaign's current state are not offered — you cannot pause something already paused. A greyed-out primary button on a finished campaign invites the click that queues several hundred people to receive email.

Inside a campaign

Opening a campaign gives five tabs. The header carries the essentials — daily limit, variant count, when it was created — with one primary button that is Pause or Resume depending on where the campaign is, and absent entirely when neither applies.

The five tabs
TabWhat is on it

SequenceThe emails, in order, with per-step performance. Variants nested under their step. Each opens to Preview, Spintax and Blueprint. Buttons to edit, copy a sequence in, or push this one out.
Copy & OfferTag this campaign's opening email against the seven dimensions, and say which offer it sells. This is what feeds the copy comparison.

The sequence editor

Editing works on a flat list, because that is what a sequence actually is — an ordered set of emails, with variants sitting at a position rather than adding one. Rows are numbered by position and a variant is labelled variant of 1 rather than being given a number of its own.

Editing tools

Part 12

Sending schedule

Everything else looks backwards. This screen looks forwards: what is going out next, and for whom.

The next three days

Three cards across the top — Today, Tomorrow, Day after — each showing that day's total and campaign count. They are the day selector and the comparison at the same time.

That matters more than it sounds. A forecast answers "what goes out today"; the question straight afterwards is always "and is that normal?" — which a control showing one number at a time cannot answer. Seeing 8.1K → 4.4K → 3.4K tells you volume is tapering before you click anything.

Per client

Below the selector, one card per client, largest first. Each shows the client's total, a bar giving their share of the day, and every campaign due to send with its own figure. "995 emails" does not tell you whether that client is most of today's sending or a rounding error against it, and that is exactly what grouping by client is for.

Campaign status is named only when it is not active. Everything with mail queued for today is active, so labelling all of them adds nothing — while a paused campaign that is still scheduled to send is precisely the thing worth catching.

Sync schedule

The forecast itself is always read live, so there is no stale number to refresh. What can lag is the naming: a campaign created in EmailBison in the last half hour may appear here unnamed and not yet attached to a client. The Sync schedule button fetches campaigns immediately and re-reads all three days.

If a day cannot be retrieved

A clear message says so. An empty forecast and a failed one look identical on screen and mean opposite things, so the failure is always stated rather than shown as "nothing scheduled".

Part 13

Clients

The quietest page in the dashboard and one of the most consequential. Every client-grouped number depends on what is set here.

Why this page exists

EmailBison has no concept of a client. It has campaigns, and the client's name happens to appear in the campaign's name — The Keyes Company + Nicole + West, C21 Results - Elite Team 2 + Nicole + FMLS. This page is where that convention is turned into a real grouping.

The match is saved, not recalculated every time you load a report. That means a correction you make here sticks, and renaming a client tomorrow does not silently rewrite last quarter's grouping.

Adding and editing a client

The client form
FieldWhat it is for
NameHow the client appears everywhere in the dashboard.
AliasesOther spellings that appear in campaign names. Adding an alias here absorbs a legacy spelling without renaming anything inside EmailBison.
Match modeNormally the name may appear anywhere in a campaign name. For a short or generic name you can tighten it to match only at the start, or only exactly.

How matching behaves

  • Whole words only. A client named Rise will never match Sunrise Realty.
  • The longest match wins. C21 Results - Elite Team beats a shorter Elite Team, so the more specific client gets the campaign.
  • A genuine tie is never guessed. If two clients match equally well the campaign is flagged for a person to resolve, rather than one being picked silently.
  • Your corrections are never overwritten. A campaign you assign by hand stays assigned, no matter how often matching re-runs.

Deleting a client does not delete its campaigns — they move to Unassigned, where they are visible and reassignable.

Unassigned campaigns

A panel lists every campaign that matched nobody, each with a picker beside it. This is the single place where a gap in the reporting gets closed, and the page states plainly that these campaigns still count toward workspace totals — they appear in the Unassigned row on the Clients view.

Excluding a campaign

Some campaigns are not client work at all — internal tests, template campaigns, scratch lists. Those can be excluded, with a reason recorded. An excluded campaign is left out of client totals entirely rather than sitting in Unassigned forever, which keeps Unassigned meaning "needs attention" rather than "background noise".

Reply groupings

Each client can be given their own set of breakdown cards on the Replies view. Brokerage, location, sales volume, MLS and top producing city are all available; tick the ones that matter for that client. Turning one off affects only that client — the default set every other client sees is left alone. A client with a custom set is marked, so an unexpected set of cards is traceable rather than mysterious.

Part 14

Keeping the numbers fresh

The dashboard keeps a local copy of everything so it can answer questions instantly. This is how that copy stays current, and what to do if it looks like it hasn't.

What updates, and how often

Thirteen background jobs, each running as often as its source actually changes rather than as often as it is cheap to run:

The refresh schedule
EveryWhat refreshesWhy that often
10 minutesRepliesThe one thing people watch for during a working day.
30 minutesCampaigns and client matchingNew campaigns should not take an hour to appear.
HourlyDaily performance figuresOne fetch returns the whole recent window, so frequent is nearly free.
HourlyReply timingA draining queue that works through new replies.
HourlyOutcomes, and matching them to campaignsResults happen on human timescales, not sending ones.
3 hoursPer-step performanceThe expensive one — hundreds of requests each run.
3 hoursInbox and domain healthSending health moves over hours, not minutes.
NightlyLead details, sequences, and four wide re-checksSee below.

The nightly deep sweeps

Overnight, four jobs re-check a much wider window than the frequent ones do. This is drift repair. EmailBison revises recent days after the fact — a bounce arrives late, an open is recorded afterwards, someone marks a reply as interested by hand — and without a wide nightly re-read, last month's numbers would slowly drift away from the source.

They run in the small hours of the workspace's own timezone, an hour apart so they never compete with each other, and always outside the sending window.

Two properties worth trusting

  • Running any job twice is always safe. Nothing is ever double-counted. A refresh that is interrupted simply re-covers the same window next time.
  • A failed run never skips a window. Progress only moves forward on success, so a bad night is re-fetched rather than lost. There is no class of "we quietly missed everything between 4:12 and 4:19".

Sync buttons

Where waiting for the next scheduled run would be frustrating, there is a button. Copy & Offer has Sync campaigns; the sending schedule has Sync schedule.

Each reports what actually changed — 101 campaigns, 40 clients, 2 unassigned, 17 excluded — rather than a bare "Synced", because "Synced" with no number cannot be told apart from a button that did nothing, which is exactly the doubt the button exists to remove. If a scheduled run happens to be in progress, it says so and leaves it to finish rather than starting a second one.

If something looks stale

  1. Check the range first. Most surprises are a filter, not a fault.
  2. Press the relevant sync button and read what it reports.
  3. Give per-step figures a few hours. Those refresh every three hours, so a campaign launched this morning may show sends before its per-step breakdown catches up.
  4. Remember recent days move. Yesterday's bounce count can rise slightly overnight. That is EmailBison correcting itself, and the nightly sweep picking the correction up.
Part 15

Conventions worth knowing

A short list of rules that hold everywhere. Knowing them removes most of the surprises.

The dash

– means no data. It never means zero. This is enforced everywhere: any figure that cannot honestly be worked out — no replies to divide by, no timings recorded yet — shows a dash. "No replies yet" and "replies answered instantly" must never look alike.

Rounded here, exact there

The headline band rounds: 272.4K, 3.7K. Tables do not: 272,389, 3,679. The band is built for scanning at a glance; tables are built for comparing rows and checking arithmetic. Digits are aligned in columns everywhere so figures can be compared down a column without reading each one.

Colour

Every chart colour belongs to the thing it measures, not to its position. Removing a line never repaints the others. Status colours — healthy watch critical — are reserved and never reused for a data series, so a red line is a metric and a red chip is a warning, always.

Minimum sample sizes

Three places refuse to rank or report on too little data, because a confident-looking number from a tiny sample is worse than no number:

Floors
WhereFloorWhy
Copy medals500 sendsCopy comparison is the easiest place to fool yourself.
Variant medals200 sendsOtherwise a variant with three sends and one positive reply wins.
Follow-up time per campaign5 repliesBelow that a "median" is one person's lunch break.

Steps and variants

Worth repeating because it appears in seven different places: a step is a position in the sequence; a variant is an alternative wording for a position that already exists. A variant is never counted as a step, never shows a wait of its own, and always appears nested under the step it replaces. Three steps with one variant reads 3 steps · 1 variant everywhere — the campaigns table, the campaign page, the editor, offer cards, suggested groups, and both copy dialogs.

One authority per family of numbers

Replies, Human Replies and Positive always come from the same place as each other, so the three figures on a row always add up. Sent and Bounces come from EmailBison's own daily figures. Prospects counts distinct people. Nothing is ever sourced two different ways in two different places, which is why the same metric never has two slightly different values on two screens.

Everything ties out. The daily chart adds up to the headline Sent. The client rows plus Unassigned add up to the headline Sent. The campaign rows add up to the headline Sent. This holds with any combination of filters applied — and Prospects is the single, documented exception, for the reason given in Part 04.
